# Direction 3 literature survey: transferring a pretrained action-conditioned dynamics prior

**Revised:** 3 November 2026  
**Question:** Can a pretrained *dynamics model that already consumes actions* transfer to a new robot with 84 demonstrations, and what action interface makes that plausible?

## Executive answer

**Yes, but the evidence is conditional.** A frozen visual encoder plus a newly learned predictor is not the same experiment as transferring pretrained action-conditioned dynamics. The strongest evidence for a transferable dynamics prior comes from models trained on varied robot interactions with explicit, temporally aligned action inputs, or from a shared latent-action interface that can be learned from videos across embodiments. Examples include V-JEPA 2-AC, CLAP, LAC-WM, A2World, RynnWorld-Latent, and the 2026 WAM systems.

The main qualification is scale and embodiment fit. V-JEPA 2-AC is the cleanest released action-conditioned predictor, but it was trained for one Franka arm with a 7D end-effector action/state interface and a fixed camera. RynnWorld-Latent is unusually close to this robot's **54-DoF dual-arm/20-DoF-hand structure**, but its world model was pretrained on about 6,190 hours and its target-robot post-training used 3,850 physical episodes. CLAP, A2World, LAC-WM, and the 2026 WAM systems also use substantial multi-robot data. **No reviewed paper establishes that an unmodified pretrained predictor will learn useful 54D bimanual dynamics from only 84 demonstrations.**

The practical opportunity is to preserve the pretrained temporal dynamics and fit a small action interface. For this setup, the useful transfer choices are:

1. **V-JEPA 2-AC as a controlled transfer probe:** map the arms' commands to its 7D end-effector interface, and explicitly address the fact that the released model represents one arm. This is the most direct test of a released action-conditioned predictor.
2. **A latent-action adapter as the robot-agnostic route:** use a cross-embodiment model such as CLAP-LAM or RynnWorld-Latent's shared action descriptors, then fit a 54D-action-to-latent adapter. This avoids requiring FK in the pretrained world-model interface, though a robot-specific low-level action decoder still has to execute the selected motion.
3. **A geometry/flow action interface:** use observed object/hand motion or 3D point flow as the shared transition representation and let an inverse controller map it to the robot. This is the cleanest embodiment-agnostic interface but is not a drop-in V-JEPA 2-AC adapter.

Do not start by optimizing CEM candidate scores against the same latent distance that already failed on the bottle task. First establish that changing the action changes the model's predicted object/hand motion in the correct direction on held-out, paired transitions. A plausible latent-distance score is not evidence of an action-faithful dynamics prior.

## Scope and reading notes

The survey distinguishes:

- **Pretrained action-conditioned dynamics:** a world model has learned an action-to-future relationship before adaptation.
- **Pretrained action-free dynamics plus an action adapter:** a temporal predictor is pretrained on video, then action conditioning is introduced for the target environment.
- **Pretrained features or predictive policy representations:** the visual trunk has a prior, but the action-conditioned predictor/action head is trained downstream. These are useful comparators, not proof that pretrained action-conditioned dynamics transfer.

I reviewed the full PDFs in the local papers_d3 directory; page references below refer to the printed PDF page numbers, including appendices. A PDF was converted to a page-marked text file where one was not already present. The abbreviated text below records the method, training setup, experiments/ablations, and limits relevant to transfer; it does not treat abstract claims as evidence for action faithfulness.

## Per-paper notes

### 1. V-JEPA 2 and V-JEPA 2-AC — the main released action-conditioned predictor

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09985), especially §3.1–3.2 (PDF pp. 9–11), §4 and §4.3 (pp. 11–15), Appendix B.1 and B.4 (pp. 37, 40–41).

**Method and training.** V-JEPA 2-AC freezes the ViT-g/16 V-JEPA 2 encoder and trains a roughly 300M-parameter autoregressive predictor: 24 transformer layers, 16 heads, width 1024. At 256×256 and patch size 16, each image contributes a 16×16 map of 1408D patch features. The predictor separately projects visual patches, action vectors, and end-effector states; it uses 3D RoPE for visual patches, temporal RoPE for action/state tokens, and block-causal attention. The paper's robot interface is **one Franka arm**, with a 7D base-relative EEF state (xyz, extrinsic Euler orientation, gripper) and a 7D action equal to the state change between adjacent sampled frames. The encoder is frozen; the predictor is trained on under 62 hours of DROID video after removing short clips, about 23k trajectories including successful and failed demonstrations. It samples 4-second clips at 4 fps (16 frames) and 256×256 RGB. Training combines teacher-forced next-latent L1 loss with a two-step autoregressive rollout loss. (Main paper §3.1, pp. 9–10; Appendix B.1, p. 37.)

**Experiments and ablations.** With image-goal MPC, the paper reports zero-shot reaching, grasping, and pick/place on two Franka arms in separate labs. Average reported success is 100% reach, 65% cup grasp, 25% box grasp, and 80%/65% cup/box pick-place in Table 2 (p. 14). Planning uses CEM over action trajectories, executes only the first action, then replans. The paper's action set is bounded; candidate trajectories are sampled from Gaussian sequences and the top-k population updates their means/variances (§3.2, pp. 10–11). For its reported planning comparison, 800 candidates × 10 refinement steps, horizon 1, took **16 seconds per action on one RTX 4090**; the action-conditioned Cosmos baseline took 4 minutes/action at 80×10, horizon 1 (Table 3, p. 15). These times are not 15 Hz control.

**Failure cases and limits.** The paper explicitly reports camera-position sensitivity. The model must infer the EEF action axes from monocular RGB without camera calibration; where the robot base is not visible this is ill-posed. The authors manually tried camera positions and retained one that worked across their tasks (§4.3, pp. 14–15). Appendix B.4 (pp. 40–41) varies camera viewing angle and reports that inferred action axes rotate with the view; predicted xy deltas differ from true robot deltas, with about 1.6 cm mean absolute error against a roughly 5 cm average commanded delta in the described sweep. Training on both left and right DROID views without camera conditioning degraded performance (Appendix B.1, p. 37). Long-horizon error accumulation, exponential search growth, and reliance on image goals are additional stated limitations (§4.3, p. 15).

**Practical release.** The [official repository](https://github.com/facebookresearch/vjepa2) publishes the V-JEPA 2-AC ViT-g checkpoint and a PyTorch Hub loader, vjepa2_ac_vit_giant; the checkpoint URL is [vjepa2-ac-vitg.pt](https://dl.fbaipublicfiles.com/vjepa2/vjepa2-ac-vitg.pt). A HEAD request on 3 Nov 2026 returned 11,760,743,310 bytes (about 11.76 decimal GB / 10.95 GiB). The repository source is MIT-licensed, but I did not find a separate, explicit checkpoint-weight licence in the repo; do not infer the weight licence from the code licence. The paper describes 16-frame clips, while the current official DROID recipe is named 8f and sets 8 frames at 4 fps, 256 crop, tubelet 2, 7D action/state, and a 24-layer/1024-width predictor. Pin the repository revision and verify checkpoint/config shapes before use; the current Hub constructor's generic default is 64 frames. The released config sets BF16 and requests 220 GB per GPU for the post-training recipe; that is a training configuration, not inference VRAM. No official inference-memory benchmark was found. (Official [README](https://github.com/facebookresearch/vjepa2/blob/main/README.md), [configuration](https://github.com/facebookresearch/vjepa2/blob/main/configs/train/vitg16/droid-256px-8f.yaml), [predictor code](https://github.com/facebookresearch/vjepa2/blob/main/src/models/ac_predictor.py), and [license](https://github.com/facebookresearch/vjepa2/blob/main/LICENSE).)

**Implication here.** This is the best direct test of a pretrained AC dynamics prior, but not a plug-in model for 54D bimanual joints. It also gives a clear falsifiable test: can a small input adapter preserve the action-to-object-motion relation under this camera, with both arms represented?

### 2. V-JEPA 2.1 — denser features, not pretrained robot dynamics

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14482), especially §3.3 and Table 6 (PDF pp. 12–13), with distillation details in Appendix (p. 34).

V-JEPA 2.1 trains dense, spatially detailed video features with a masked latent-prediction/distillation objective. It does **not** take robot actions in its pretrained interface and is not a pretrained AC world model. For its robot planning comparison, the authors freeze the V-JEPA 2.1 encoder and train a new roughly 300M frame-causal predictor on raw DROID; the predictor uses teacher forcing plus a two-step rollout objective (§3.3, p. 12). Table 6 reports V-JEPA 2 at 100% reach / 60% grasp / 80% pick-place (800 CEM samples, 10 iterations, horizon 1, 3 s/action); V-JEPA 2.1 with the same setting reaches 100% / 70% / 80%; V-JEPA 2.1 at horizon 8 with 300 samples and 15 iterations reaches 100% / 80% / 80% at 14 s/action (p. 13). The paper identifies early gripper closing or opening in transit as failure causes, even where spatial understanding is adequate (p. 13). This is evidence that V-JEPA 2.1 features can support a newly trained robot predictor, **not** that its features transfer action-conditioned dynamics. The appendix's 12-block teacher distillation is a representation-compression procedure, not an action-adapter recipe (p. 34). The paper is therefore an important negative control for the Oct 29–30 setup.

### 3. V-JEPA Policy — future features help a policy, but action dynamics are learned downstream

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37250), §§3–5 (PDF pp. 4–9) and appendices (pp. 14–17).

This follow-up uses a frozen V-JEPA 2.1 image encoder and trains a predictor/policy system; it does not transfer the V-JEPA 2-AC predictor. Its future predictor and action expert are separate 24-layer transformers (widths 1024 and 512); the action expert reads future-informed context keys/values, and both parts are jointly trained downstream while the visual and T5 encoders stay frozen (§3, pp. 4–5; Appendix A.2, p. 14). The target predictor is initially trained on DROID videos, instructions, and proprioception for 100k steps / batch 192, without action labels; downstream adaptation transfers that predictor and initializes the action expert from scratch (Appendix E, pp. 16–17). LIBERO training uses 1,693 demonstrations. The real TianJi Marvin setup has four RGB views and 16D action output (7 arm joints + 1 gripper state per arm), which is materially different from this robot's 54D joint/hand commands (Appendices A.1 and B, pp. 14–15). The paper reports 55%/35% real success from scratch and 75%/85% after predictor-only DROID pretraining on Table Cleanup/Saucer Racking (Table 3, p. 7); the ablation also shows future-loss gains (Table 9, p. 16). The action expert remains a target-trained policy component. The reported 178 ms and 4.66 GiB core inference profile is on RTX 4090 and excludes sensor I/O and actuator overhead (Table 6, p. 9; Appendix F, p. 17). This is evidence for a useful action-free temporal prior and joint policy-training recipe, not small-data transfer of an AC dynamics checkpoint.

### 4. JEPA-WAM — joint-embedding transition supervision paired with policy learning

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09381), §§3–5 (PDF pp. 3–6) and controlled ablations in the appendix (pp. 14–18).

JEPA-WAM uses a frozen V-JEPA 2.1 encoder and predicts a dense joint-embedding transition from current and future images. A shared Qwen2.5-0.5B predictor also feeds a flow-matching action expert, so transition loss and action generation share learned context; the future target encoder/predictor can be removed at deployment (main paper §§3 and 4, pp. 3–5; Appendix A.5, p. 12). Training spans LIBERO and RoboTwin; real evaluation uses a two-arm AgileX platform and about 100 demonstrations per task. Reported LIBERO+ results improve with JEPA transition loss, including further gains when added to pretrained π0.5; controlled ablations compare joint current/future tokens with future-only or feature-difference targets (main results p. 5; appendix pp. 14–18). The method improves policies with predictive supervision, but it does not supply a pretrained action-conditioned transition predictor for a new embodiment. The transition target is used as training supervision rather than an explicit action-to-future model at deployment. The paper's timing and benchmark results should not be conflated with V-JEPA 2-AC latency or input requirements.

### 5. DINO-WM — a useful diagnostic baseline, not a pretrained dynamics model

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04983), §3 (PDF pp. 3–5), evaluation and ablations in Appendix A (pp. 13–18).

DINO-WM freezes DINOv2 patch features and trains its own task-specific visual dynamics predictor. It embeds actions with an MLP and concatenates the action embedding to visual patch tokens; the predictor uses causal attention and a teacher-forced latent prediction loss. Experiments use simulated PushT, point-maze, wall, rope, and granular tasks with small task-specific datasets; the paper varies history length, data volume, causal masking, and model/reconstruction choices. Its causal-mask ablation is especially instructive: without masking, longer history performs worse (PushT success falls from about .92 at three masked history frames to .08 without the mask; Appendix A.4, p. 14). Scaling PushT data from 200 to 18,500 samples also changes success markedly (p. 15). Planning can be expensive (CEM timing in Appendix, p. 17), and open-loop visual error compounds. DINO-WM demonstrates how to train a useful AC predictor on frozen features; it does not establish transfer of pretrained dynamics, and its action concatenation resembles the kind of weak input path that should be stress-tested for action sensitivity.

### 6. DINO-world — pretrained action-free dynamics plus zero-initialized action blocks

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19468), §§3–4 and Table 4 (PDF pp. 4–9).

DINO-world pretrains a large DINOv2-based predictor on about 66M web videos without actions, then adds a zero-initialized action residual block at each predictor layer and fine-tunes on action-labelled simulated tasks. Actions are supplied per frame; the paper uses a frame skip of five in its control experiments. Table 4 compares training only the action blocks with fine-tuning the whole model: on PointMaze, PushT, and Wall, the action-block-only update improves over the scratch action-conditioned baseline, while full-model tuning improves further (p. 9). This is a strong example of **frozen temporal predictor + small action adapter**, but the pretrained world model itself was action-free. There is no real robot or morphology-transfer experiment. For this project, it is a more meaningful comparison than adding a new action head to V-JEPA features because the backbone has learned temporal prediction, though action grounding still has to be acquired from the robot data.

### 7. LAC-WM — shared latent actions with a small explicit-action projector

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10846), §§3–5 (PDF pp. 3–9), model/training appendices (pp. 12–17).

LAC-WM learns a shared latent action from frame transitions and trains a forward dynamics model to predict future visual embeddings from that latent. A motion decoder grounds the latent to physical labels such as end-effector/camera pose. Cross-augmentation feeds independently augmented views to the inverse and forward models to reduce a shortcut in which the latent simply carries future appearance. For transfer, a small projector maps a new robot's explicit action into the pretrained latent space. The paper uses a three-stage adaptation recipe: LoRA rank 2 on the inverse/forward components, then freeze the forward model and learn the action projector, then jointly update the projector and rank-2 forward LoRA (Appendix A, pp. 13–15). Pretraining uses 50k trajectories each from EgoDex, AgiBot, and DROID; target transfer includes simulated dexterous tasks and LIBERO, not physical robot deployment. LAC-WM improves its reported unseen-category/instance and LIBERO results over explicit-action baselines, but absolute success on the difficult dexterous setting remains modest (§4–5, pp. 5–9). Pretraining used 64 H200 GPUs for four days and inference timings are batch/model specific (Appendix, p. 15). Its latent-space projector is a good design reference; reproducing its pretraining is outside the present compute budget.

### 8. CLAP — cross-embodiment action-conditioned video models with two useful bridges

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27406), §§3–5 (PDF pp. 4–14), action-interface/inference appendices (pp. 21–26). Code and checkpoints are linked from the [official repository](https://github.com/omni-CLAP/clap).

CLAP uses a video diffusion model conditioned by per-frame action tokens. Its action choices are (i) 7D end-effector pose/gripper, (ii) per-frame language actions, or (iii) a learned 32D latent action. Critically, each predicted frame attends to its own corresponding action token; the paper says this per-frame alignment is needed for fine-grained controllability (§3.2, pp. 5–6). The latent-action model is trained as an inverse-dynamics model from video transitions and supports human-video pretraining; CLAP-CURR then moves from latent actions to a physical action space. The video model uses six history and five future frames; training drops action context with probability 5% for classifier-free guidance (Appendix B.2, p. 21). The reported mixture combines OXE robot datasets and EgoDex; the world-model backbone is a Stable Video Diffusion UNet and the VAE/CLIP components are frozen (Appendix B.2 and C.1, pp. 21–22).

For adaptation, the paper trains a zero-initialized MLP from EEF actions to 32D latent actions while freezing CLAP-LAM; it also fine-tunes CLAP-CURR on the 14D YAM bimanual and 26D G1 action spaces (§4.1 and Appendix C.1, pp. 7, 14, 22). Prediction metrics and physical cross-policy planning are reported, but the paper does not test a 54D hand-joint interface. Inference is under 12 GB VRAM; 11 frames with 25 denoising steps take 1.49 s on H200 (Appendix C.2, p. 22). It is a practical candidate for adapter experiments, not a 15 Hz world model. The paper also reports hallucinations and high training/inference cost as limits (§5, p. 14).

### 9. A2World — the most direct large-scale evidence for action-grounded dynamics pretraining

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29501), §§3–4 (PDF pp. 4–9), results and ablations (pp. 12–22).

A2World starts from Cosmos Predict and pretrains a multi-view video diffusion world model on 2.1M+ robot trajectories across more than 20 embodiments. Each arm is represented by a 7D end-effector pose/gripper action; the action chunk enters the diffusion timestep and modulates DiT layers with AdaLN scale/shift/gates (§3.1, pp. 4–6). The 2.5B-parameter base predicts future video from an initial view and action sequence; downstream A2World-sim and A2World-policy reuse those weights (§4, pp. 8–9). The reported recipe uses 64 H200 GPUs for the base, 32 H200 GPUs for policy transfer, and 8 H200 GPUs for simulation fine-tuning. Simulation and real-robot results compare against generic/text-video initializations and show benefits from action-to-video pretraining (Tables, pp. 12–15). This is strong evidence for the central premise, but not evidence that 84 demos can substitute for action-rich multi-robot pretraining. Its 7D-per-arm EEF interface also requires either a dual-arm extension or an adapter for this hand-heavy command space.

### 10. τ0-WM — action/video consistency at scale

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01027), method, experiments, and appendices (PDF pp. 3–12).

τ0-WM couples a 5B Wan2.2-TI2V video branch to a 0.5B action DiT and an action-conditioned video simulator (ACVS). ACVS conditions candidate future rollouts through action projections added to diffusion-time and AdaLN modulation embeddings, then predicts video and dense task-progress reward; failure-heavy and recovery trajectories are intentionally used to improve off-distribution action evaluation (§§IV–V, pp. 4–6). Pretraining uses 27.3k hours of robot, UMI-style, and egocentric data; global batch is 12,288 in pretraining and 384 in downstream post-training (Appendix A, p. 10). Test-time compute first filters candidate actions with a re-denoising consistency score, and only invokes ACVS plus low-quality action rectification when candidates fail a threshold (pp. 6–7). On two strict single-attempt tasks, RCS improves average success from .43 to .50 and RCS+LAR to .60; heterogeneous UMI/ego pretraining improves zero-shot average from .14 to .55 in the reported probe (Tables I–II, pp. 8–9). The model spans three embodiments, but its data scale and 220 ms/query RTX 5090 latency (180 ms cached; 140 ms with compile) exceed the current setting (Appendix A, p. 10). It shows how action-conditioned rollout scoring can be combined with failure data, but it does not establish few-shot transfer of an AC checkpoint to a new 54D robot.

### 11. OPENWAM — composable dynamics components and the importance of counterfactual transitions

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07922), §§3–5 (PDF pp. 4–9), implementation and dynamics appendices (pp. 14–18). This is distinct from the OpenWAM-Alpha pretraining study below.

OPENWAM initializes a causal video backbone from Wan2.2-5B and continues training on over 14,000 nominal hours of robot/human/synthetic video without action labels (Appendix B, pp. 15–16). It then compares joint, video-then-action, action-then-video, and decoupled video/action generation in a common mixture-of-transformers architecture. Its action branch uses 7D EEF position/axis-angle/gripper chunks and proprioception (§4.1, p. 7; Appendix A.1, p. 14). The paper also trains local inverse/forward dynamics components separately, allowing a frozen inverse model to consume a future visual trajectory from a different adapted predictor.

The key result for this task is not just its high native policy score. From 500 LIBERO demonstrations, the authors generate 32,000 local counterfactual segments / about 4.1M control records, 29.7× the demonstration control count (Appendix C, pp. 16–17). A frozen local-context IDM trained on those branches transfers across four held-out LIBERO-90 targets at 84.0% mean success; a demo-only local IDM reaches 21.5%, while a full-context demo/mixed IDM averages 44.5/47.0% (Table 5, p. 9). Forward-dynamics outcome identification among 16 alternatives rises from 21.1% with demo-only supervision to 71.3% with counterfactual-only supervision (Table 7, p. 9). This is direct evidence that successful demos alone can fail to teach which action causes which local outcome. The transfer evidence is simulation-only; real-robot policy experiments use 200 demonstrations per task, not 84, and 7D actions (Appendix A.5, p. 15). Long-horizon physical transfer remains open (p. 9).

### 12. OpenWAM-Alpha — action capacity, representation, and camera robustness

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07398), model studies and alpha results (PDF pp. 10–24 and appendices pp. 40–43).

This work describes a systematic pretraining study and an OpenWAM-Alpha model trained on roughly 6,369 hours / 518.5M frames from 21 human/robot embodiments. It uses a Wan2.2-TI2V-5B video model plus a dedicated ~1B action DiT and an 80D common API: two 34D arm slots (xyz, 6D rotation, gripper, 24 hand joints each) plus 12 reserved dimensions, with per-dataset masks for missing coordinates (pp. 10, 17–20). The paper's controlled studies compare backbone sizes, action capacity, video/action information flow, representation compression, and human/robot mixtures (pp. 11–16). Larger/more capable action branches and the correct information flow matter; raw V-JEPA 2.1/DINOv3 feature tokens work better after the paper's S-VAE compression, and co-training human plus robot data improves an OOD setting. Its alpha model reports about 170 ms per action chunk on RTX 5090 (p. 19).

The relevant caveat is its LIBERO-Plus camera/noise weakness: the paper reports large drops in camera and noise perturbations and attributes them to camera coverage and pixel-level prediction sensitivity (pp. 22–23). Its data and model scale are far beyond 84 target demos. Still, the 80D masked action tokenizer and explicit 24D hand slots are closer to this robot than single-arm EEF interfaces.

### 13. DreamZero — large action-video co-training and close-embodiment few-shot transfer

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15922), §§3–6 (PDF pp. 6–19), timing/data details (Appendix, p. 21).

DreamZero is a 14B Wan2.1 I2V world-action model that jointly denoises video and action sequences, adds state/action encoders, and inserts executed observations into the KV cache after each chunk. Its core pretraining data is about 500 hours of AgiBot experience over 22 environments/7,193 episodes; it trains on relative joint positions and fine-tunes all DiT/action/state parameters, freezing text/image encoders and VAE (§4, pp. 10–12). The authors report LoRA as suboptimal in their setting (p. 11). It produces 48-step chunks at 30 Hz; the reported accelerated system reaches about 150 ms/chunk on dual GB200, far beyond the current H20 envelope (p. 8).

Cross-embodiment few-shot tests use 12 minutes of human video or 20 minutes of YAM robot video, and a new YAM adaptation uses 55 trajectories/30 minutes (§4, pp. 15–17). Those are promising results, but the robots are close bimanual embodiments and the main model receives 500 hours of robot data. The paper attributes most failures to video prediction rather than action extraction and notes memory/latency and precision limitations (pp. 14, 17–19). It is a policy-scale result, not a small target-data dynamics adapter.

### 14. RynnWorld-Latent — a new latent-action prior with an unusually close 54D robot

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36791), §§3–5 and Tables 1–7 (PDF pp. 4–17); [official project](https://alibaba-damo-academy.github.io/RynnWorld-Latent.github.io), [code](https://github.com/alibaba-damo-academy/RynnWorld-Latent), [model page](https://huggingface.co/Alibaba-DAMO-Academy/RynnWorld-Latent).

This paper is especially relevant because it evaluates a Marvin-WUJI platform with **two 7-DoF arms and two 20-DoF hands (54 DoF)** (p. 10). Its cross-embodiment pretraining uses a frozen 3D-grounded RynnLAM representation extracted from transitions: 512D motion tokens + 64D global action latent + 32D camera component. The 576D manipulation portion and 32D camera portion are quantile-normalized. It trains a Cosmos3-Edge video transformer on 23 datasets, 553.5M frames, 2.57M episodes, and about 6,190 hours (pp. 4–6). The model combines global action tokens with identity-initialized frame-aligned local modulation; the local action is aligned to the future latent it should affect (§3.3, p. 6). Training drops action conditioning for CFG (10% whole sequence, plus independent 10% drops for motion and camera slices), with guidance scale 1.15 (§3.4, pp. 6–7).

For target adaptation, it trains a new target-robot action encoder that maps each native action interval into the model's conditioning stream (§3.5, pp. 7–8). The target-robot corpus is 3,850 real episodes across six tasks, with 5% held out (p. 7). On its dual-arm platforms, it reports improved visual prediction metrics over the listed baselines; its fixed-initial-frame, changed-latent-action test is qualitative (Tables 2–3, pp. 11–12; Fig. 5, p. 13). Ablations support latent-action pretraining and frame-aligned injection (Table 3, pp. 11, 16). Its synthetic data engine creates about 800 hours of robot-labelled videos and improves policy success in its evaluated settings (pp. 14–15). The paper warns that latent actions may include camera/appearance changes, visual metrics do not establish physical correctness, and generated frames and retargeted action labels can be inconsistent (pp. 16–17). This is a compelling model family and action-interface reference for the 54D robot, but it is not evidence that 84 real demos suffice.

### 15. 3DFlowAction — a robot-agnostic motion/action interface

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06199), §§3–5 (PDF pp. 3–10), training details (Appendix, p. 16).

3DFlowAction represents manipulation as 3D point flow rather than robot joints. It builds a large video dataset from DROID, Bridge, RH20T, HOI4D, RT-1, AgiWorld, and LIBERO, extracts object/gripper tracks, removes camera motion, lifts tracks with depth, and trains a video motion model with LoRA. At test time it optimizes end-effector SE(3) sequences to match a desired flow; gripper poses and IK are used to convert the selected flow into robot commands (§§3–4, pp. 3–9). The real-robot tasks use about 30 human demonstrations/task (~10 minutes), and the paper reports gains over video/planning baselines on four tasks; an ablation without large-scale pretraining falls from 70% to 30%, and removing its closed-loop rendered-flow check falls to 50% (pp. 7–9). It transfers without robot-specific training in the reported setup. Limits include flexible objects/occlusion and dependence on point tracking, depth, segmentation, and a robot controller (p. 10). It is an embodiment-agnostic target interface, but not a drop-in V-JEPA 2-AC action vector.

### 16. XEWorld — geometry explains why raw cross-robot actions fail

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05799), §§3–6 (PDF pp. 3–8) and few-shot/ablation appendices (pp. 12–16).

XEWorld tests action-conditioned video models on held-out simulated robot bodies. It fine-tunes a Wan2.2-TI2V-5B model with video and several action renderings: a 16D canonical joint vector, ray-map, robot masks, and robot-masked optical flow. Its experiments include five bimanual bodies, 25 tasks, and held-out embodiments; the appendix describes 50 trajectories per training robot/task, BF16 gradient checkpointing, and one 8-GPU node (p. 12). Kinematic/rendered action maps transfer better than a joint vector alone; per-frame FK renderings improve results, and held-out performance tracks appearance/kinematic similarity. One-, two-, and three-demo fine-tunes recover some performance but can catastrophically forget a seen robot (pp. 12–16). This is simulation evidence, not real-world transfer. It argues that a numerical joint coordinate is not a robot-agnostic action meaning: if raw joint-space input is retained, add embodiment-aware kinematic alignment or learn an adapter.

### 17. Dream4ACT — rendering the robot action into a shared visual interface

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40153), §§3–5 (PDF pp. 4–8), latency/limitations appendix (pp. 16–17).

Dream4ACT renders target joint actions as multi-view virtual robot trajectories using the robot URDF, then learns video-action behavior with a pretrained Wan2.2 model. At inference a geometric optimizer uses rendered-view matches to solve for joint targets. It trains across five embodiments and real experiments with 60 demos per embodiment/task pair (720 total); on RoboTwin the reported model is competitive but not best on all metrics (§4, pp. 6–8). The robot action interface is carried by rendering and kinematics, not a universal raw joint index. The method depends on matching URDF/render conventions, kinematic accuracy, and geometric observability. Real inference is about 6.9 s for video plus 2.7 s for the action solve, with full chunks executed, so it is not a high-frequency controller (Appendix, pp. 16–17). It is a possible embodiment-aware bridge, not an H20-friendly 15 Hz prior.

### 18. OSCAR — skeleton-conditioned world model

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04463), §§3–4 (PDF pp. 4–8), ablations in Table 3 (p. 7).

OSCAR adapts Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B to predict action-conditioned videos using rendered 2D robot/human skeletons as the shared control signal. The skeleton comes from kinematics and camera geometry; the paper compares it with latent-action and mesh-style conditions. It fine-tunes first on robot clips and then adds human-video warm-start data; Table 3 reports skeleton/mesh conditions outperforming the tested latent-action variant, and human warm-start helps (p. 7). The model is evaluated as a visual dynamics predictor, not as a real-robot action policy. The paper notes camera calibration limits how much data can be used and that predicted video still has limitations (§5, p. 8). It supports explicit geometric action rendering as a cross-embodiment route, but does not eliminate calibration or target-action conversion.

### 19. VPP — predictive video features as a policy input

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14803), §§4–6 (PDF pp. 4–9), ablations and real-world results (pp. 6–8).

VPP fine-tunes Stable Video Diffusion on human and robot manipulation videos, then uses a single forward pass through the video model's upsampling features as a predictive visual representation. A video former compresses the features and a diffusion-policy head learns the inverse mapping to actions. The model is not action-conditioned at the world-model level. Training uses 193,690 human and 179,074 robot manipulation trajectories plus downstream data; video fine-tuning takes 2–3 days on 8 A100s and policy fitting 6–12 hours on 4 A100s (§5.1, pp. 5–6). It reports 4.33 average task length on CALVIN ABC-D and strong real task results; with 10% CALVIN data it reaches 3.25 versus GR-1's 1.41 (Table 1, p. 5). Ablations show removal of internet data, task video, SVD pretraining, feature aggregation, or video former reduces performance (Tables 3–4, pp. 6–7). It supports predictive features for a policy, but does not test whether an AC dynamics prior transfers to a new action space.

### 20. VPP2 — a large pretrained video model plus inverse-dynamics action expert

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10270), §§2–5 (PDF pp. 2–9), data and training appendices.

VPP2 continues pretraining Wan2.1-I2V-14B on a large curated manipulation-video mixture with detailed captions and event-level trajectories, distills a fixed-horizon visual planner, then trains a mixture-of-transformers action expert conditioned on predicted futures. This is a powerful video/policy transfer system, but the action expert learns an inverse mapping; the video foundation is not an already action-conditioned dynamics checkpoint. The paper reports zero-shot real ALOHA success of 58.5% across 10 task categories versus 40.0% for π0.5 and 20.5% for Fast-WAM, plus LIBERO-Pro/OOD/RoboDojo results after benchmark post-training (§1–3, pp. 1–6). Its data pipeline explicitly aligns end-effector frames and views across source datasets (pp. 3–4). The paper's limitations discussion emphasizes that generic video models can hallucinate motion and action components can damage generalization (pp. 2, 9). Useful evidence for video-to-action policy learning, not a lightweight 84-demo dynamics adapter.

### 21. Cosmos Policy — action generation through pretrained video latent frames

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16163), §§3–6 (PDF pp. 3–10) and appendices.

Cosmos Policy fine-tunes Cosmos-Predict2-2B in one stage without architectural changes. It inserts robot proprioception, action chunks, predicted future observations, and value estimates as latent frames in the video diffusion sequence; the sequence models only the state at the start and end of one action chunk, with no input history (§4.1, pp. 4–5). Training uses half the batch for demonstration policy learning and half for world/value learning from a rollout dataset; for RoboCasa, it reports 50 human demos/task (p. 7). The model jointly predicts action/state/value on target robot data, and can use best-of-N candidate action/future/value rollouts for planning (§4, pp. 4–7). Ablations show auxiliary state/value objectives and video pretraining help; the from-scratch version drops average LIBERO success by 3.9 points (p. 9). On difficult real ALOHA tasks, post-training on 648 policy rollouts raises average score by 12.5 points, but search takes about 5 seconds/action chunk and the paper notes dependence on rollout data and one-layer best-of-N planning (pp. 10–11). This is a target-policy fine-tuning method, not a pretrained action-conditioned dynamics prior. Its results do not show that the action-to-future branch is independently reusable after a change in action space. It is a reasonable policy baseline if target data are sufficient, but it does not answer the central transfer question.

### 22. WorldGym — action-sweep validation for a learned video world model

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.00613), §§3–5 (PDF pp. 3–9), including the action-control and policy-evaluation experiments.

WorldGym trains an autoregressive action-conditioned diffusion world model across robot datasets. Each per-frame action vector is projected into the diffusion timestep embedding and applied through AdaLN-Zero; actions are randomly dropped per video clip and classifier-free guidance is used to make the model obey action inputs (§3.1, p. 3). The evaluation checks generated rollouts against held-out real robot videos and uses hard-coded one-axis-at-a-time end-effector sweeps to test direction/gripper control (pp. 4–5). In policy evaluation, predicted scores correlate with real success at r=.78 and mean policy success differs by an average of 3.3 points (pp. 5–7). The authors explicitly caution that object interactions are not always realistic and the model is an evaluation environment, not a fully physical simulator (§6, p. 9). The action-sweep protocol is a better first test for this project than candidate-scoring MPC. The model itself was trained as a broad world model; the result is not evidence of transferring an off-the-shelf predictor with 84 demos.

### 23. What Does World Modeling Actually Improve in Robot Action Learning?

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34593), §§3–6 (PDF pp. 4–10), controls and per-task tables in appendices (pp. 12–16).

This paper isolates future-video loss within two WAM families while holding architecture, initialization, action data, and training steps fixed. Predictive supervision improves success on seen-clean conditions, but it is not consistently beneficial under unseen tasks or randomized conditions (§4–6, pp. 4–9). A training-only lightweight future predictor raises the tested π0.5 success from 82.94% to 89.19% (p. 9 / Table 4), with the predictor removed at inference (appendix, pp. 14–15). The authors also analyze behavior modes and note that aggregate gains can arise through higher success within an existing mode rather than more diverse behavior (pp. 5, 8–9). This is evidence that future prediction can be useful supervision; it does not show that a model's predictions are action-faithful or transferable. It reinforces the need for counterfactual action tests, rather than relying on policy RMSE or video loss alone.

### 24. V-JEPA v1 — representation prediction, not action-conditioned dynamics

[Full paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.08471), §§2–4 (PDF pp. 3–8) and appendices.

V-JEPA v1 predicts masked video representations and studies learned visual representations. It has no robot action input, no robot action-conditioned predictor, no robot action-space adaptation, and no action-planning experiment. It is included to make the feature/dynamics distinction explicit: a pretrained video representation can contain temporal information without having learned how a new robot's controls alter that representation.

## Why our V-JEPA 2.1 attempt failed — what the literature suggests

The supplied results are consistent with an **action-grounding bottleneck**, not evidence against pretrained video features in general:

- The Oct 29–30 V-JEPA 2.1 setup froze a visual encoder and trained a new action predictor. The model had no pretrained action-to-future mapping to transfer. V-JEPA 2.1's own robot-planning section also freezes its encoder and trains a separate predictor (V-JEPA 2.1 §3.3, pp. 12–13). V-JEPA 2.1 is a dense visual feature model, not V-JEPA 2-AC.
- The approximately 0.04% gain over action-free prediction, 0.28% over persistence, +0.021% correct-action next-frame gain, 0.001-rad token-shuffle effect, and 0.458 candidate ranking reported by the team mean that the model's outcome ranking was barely controlled by actions. The candidate-scoring result—16 candidates all pushed the bottle away—is a direct warning against trusting a seemingly reasonable latent score.
- Frozen feature tokens can preserve semantics and appearance while leaving contact timing, gripper state, and object consequences weakly identified. A loss over all visual tokens can be minimized by predicting static background and persistence. This is a plausible interpretation, not a demonstrated diagnosis of the team's exact implementation.
- DINO-WM shows that action conditioning and causal attention must both be tested: the causal-mask/history ablation changes planning success sharply (Appendix A.4, p. 14). DINO-world shows that inserting zero-initialized action blocks into a pretrained temporal model gives a measurable route to adaptation (Table 4, p. 9), but only in three simulation tasks. CLAP explicitly aligns each predicted frame with its own action token (§3.2, pp. 5–6); a shared or temporally misaligned action signal is harder to use.
- V-JEPA 2-AC does more than reuse features: it gives action/state their own projected tokens, interleaves them with visual latents under a causal attention mask, and trains both teacher-forced and autoregressive rollout losses (§3.1, pp. 9–10). It also reports camera-sensitive control axes and long-horizon error (Appendix B.4, pp. 40–41; §4.3, p. 15); even this actual AC prior has limits.
- The more directly relevant evidence points to **action diversity and alignment**. OPENWAM finds that demonstration-only local dynamics are weak on alternative actions; counterfactual transitions raise 16-way outcome identification from 21.1% to 71.3% and held-out transfer success from 21.5% to about 84% in its simulated setup (Tables 5 and 7, p. 9). RynnWorld-Latent aligns each action descriptor with its corresponding future video latent and reports that removing frame-aligned conditioning worsens prediction (Table 3, pp. 11, 16). Both papers still use much more data than 84 bottle demos.
- Timing and dimensionality are also plausible causes here. The robot issues 54D joint commands at 15 Hz in 10-step chunks, while V-JEPA 2-AC was trained on one-arm 7D Cartesian deltas at 4 Hz. A 10-step chunk spans about 0.67 s, or about 2.7 V-JEPA 2-AC frame intervals. Unless commands are synchronized and resampled deliberately, a model can see an action token that is weakly aligned to the visible transition. A 54D joint vector also makes it easy for a small adapter to learn dominant correlations while missing the few hand/arm dimensions that determine contact.
- The team's token shuffling result makes spatial grounding especially important. If a policy changes by only 0.001 rad when spatial tokens are shuffled, the policy is likely using a global/pooled signal or an action prior that does not depend on the object-contact geometry it needs. The result should be treated as an empirical warning about that model/policy path, not as a claim that V-JEPA spatial features are universally uninformative.
- Finally, predictive supervision can improve a policy without making a reusable, action-faithful simulator. The controlled WAM-loss study finds task-dependent gains and no consistent advantage under shifts (§6, pp. 8–10). JEPA-WAM and V-JEPA Policy also couple future representation learning to a downstream action expert; neither proves transfer of a pretrained action-conditioned predictor.

### Most likely explanation to test first

The current evidence is most consistent with one or more of these: (1) actions are misaligned or weakly represented relative to the image transition; (2) the action interface mixes two arms and 40 hand coordinates into an undertrained mapping; (3) the latent loss rewards image persistence more than object/contact motion; and (4) the training demonstrations do not contain enough paired alternatives from similar states to identify what a different action would do. These explanations should be separated with paired, same-initial-state action interventions and held-out rollouts before additional CEM search.

## What the literature says about making actions matter

There is no clean, same-backbone, same-data benchmark proving that AdaLN/FiLM is universally better than action-token concatenation. The papers change data, objectives, and model scale together. The useful evidence is instead about **where the action enters, how it aligns with the predicted time step, whether the model must use it, and whether training includes alternative outcomes**.

| Conditioning pattern | Examples | Evidence and limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Action embedding concatenated to visual tokens | DINO-WM | Simple, low-cost entry point. Action-insensitivity remains possible; the paper's causal-mask ablation shows attention masks also matter (Appendix A.4, p. 14). |
| Separate action/state tokens interleaved with visual tokens | V-JEPA 2-AC | Preserves a dedicated action path and temporal ordering; paired with teacher-forcing and a rollout loss. Still camera-sensitive and trained on one EEF arm. |
| Per-frame cross-attention to its matching action token | CLAP | Authors emphasize frame-to-action alignment for controllability (§3.2, pp. 5–6); their action dropout is 5% for classifier-free guidance (Appendix B.2, p. 21). |
| Zero-initialized action residual blocks across predictor layers | DINO-world | A small adapter can be trained while preserving the pretrained video predictor; action-block-only and full-model fine-tuning both improve simulated prediction (Table 4, p. 9). |
| Action mapped into timestep embedding and AdaLN-Zero/scale-shift gates | WorldGym, A2World, τ0-WM | Injects action influence throughout the DiT rather than relying on a single input concatenation. WorldGym uses whole-clip action dropout and CFG, then verifies control with action sweeps (p. 3–5). These papers do not isolate AdaLN versus concatenation under matched conditions. |
| Global action token plus zero-initialized, frame-aligned scale/shift modulation (FiLM-style) | RynnWorld-Latent | Combines global temporal context and a local path targeted at the future latent affected by that action; removing frame-aligned injection degrades prediction (Table 3, pp. 11, 16). |

**Action dropout.** WorldGym drops the action condition for whole clips and uses classifier-free guidance; CLAP drops it with 5% probability; RynnWorld-Latent independently drops the full action sequence, manipulation slice, or camera slice at 10% probabilities (§3.4, pp. 6–7). These recipes train a usable unconditional branch and allow guided inference. The papers do not provide a controlled sweep proving an optimal dropout rate; too much dropping could plausibly weaken the conditioned branch, so action sensitivity must still be measured directly.

**Teacher forcing and rollout loss.** V-JEPA 2-AC uses teacher-forced next-step prediction plus a two-step autoregressive rollout loss (§3.1, pp. 9–10). DINO-WM uses a causal mask and teacher-forced latent prediction; its ablation warns that future leakage can make apparent prediction success misleading (Appendix A.4, p. 14). OPENWAM's sequential video/action programs train the second stage with the ground-truth first-stage trajectory but condition on a generated trajectory at inference (Appendix A.3, p. 15), leaving an exposure gap. For this task, include short closed-loop rollouts in evaluation, not just teacher-forced next-frame loss.

**Frame skip and action duration.** V-JEPA 2-AC samples at 4 fps and labels the action between adjacent sampled frames; DINO-WM uses a frame skip of five; CLAP harmonizes per-dataset frame skip and gives each predicted frame its own action token (Appendix C.1, p. 22). Larger skips make action effects more visible above image noise, but can hide contact timing; smaller skips preserve timing but may make object movement tiny relative to the visual loss. The correct sampling interval for the 15 Hz command stream is an empirical design choice.

**What is supported.** The best cross-paper signal is not that one injection layer wins. It is that the action must be synchronized to the future frame/transition it causes, have enough capacity to affect multiple layers or tokens, and be tested on deliberately varied actions from the same initial state. OPENWAM's counterfactual results (Tables 5 and 7, p. 9) are the strongest evidence that outcome diversity can matter more than merely adding a prediction loss. None of these results prove that a particular architecture will solve bottle grasping with 84 demonstrations.

FiLM-style scale/shift modulation and AdaLN are related feature-wise modulation mechanisms, but no reviewed paper runs a matched ablation of FiLM/AdaLN against action concatenation on the same pretrained predictor and data. The evidence supports testing injection depth and temporal alignment, not declaring a universal winner.

## Small-data adaptation recipes found in the papers

| Recipe | Source evidence | What is frozen/trainable | Transfer boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero-initialized action blocks in a pretrained temporal model | DINO-world, Table 4, p. 9 | First train only the new per-layer action blocks; compare with full-model tuning | Simulated single-arm tasks; action-free temporal pretraining |
| Small physical-action-to-latent MLP | CLAP, Appendix C.1–C.2, pp. 22–23 | Freeze CLAP-LAM/world model; learn a zero-initialized action projector from EEF to 32D latent action | DROID and Bridge; also 14D YAM/26D G1 adaptation, not 54D dexterous hands |
| Rank-2 LoRA plus action projector | LAC-WM, Appendix A, pp. 13–15 | LoRA on action/world components, then freeze forward model and learn projector, then update projector + forward LoRA | Simulation; pretraining was 64 H200 for four days |
| Separate target-robot action encoder | RynnWorld-Latent, §3.5, pp. 7–8 | Keep shared latent world model; train an embodiment-specific action encoder | 3,850 target episodes and 800-hour synthetic-data pipeline in the reported study |
| Frozen local inverse-dynamics component | OPENWAM, Table 5, p. 9 | Freeze source IDM; let a task-adapted video predictor provide future trajectories | Strong in LIBERO only with counterfactual local-transition training; physical transfer untested |
| V-JEPA 2-AC input-adapter transfer | No target-robot adapter experiment in the paper | Proposed here: begin with input/state adapters; consider rank-2 LoRA only after frozen-prior action tests | Hypothesis for 84 demos; must be reported as a new experiment, not a published precedent |

The best supported small-update pattern is to freeze the temporal predictor and train a zero-initialized, frame-aligned action interface first. LoRA is a second step when the interface alone is insufficient. Fine-tuning the entire visual/dynamics trunk immediately removes the main experimental control: whether useful dynamics were already in the pretrained prior.

## How to adopt a pretrained dynamics prior

### Option A — test V-JEPA 2-AC as the actual action-conditioned prior

This is the cleanest experiment for the central question because the predictor is already pretrained with actions and states. It should be treated as an action-faithfulness study first, not as a policy-ranking system.

1. Download the official 11.76 GB checkpoint and pin the repo commit/config. Start with 256×256 RGB and a fixed Orbbec view; the paper does not use RGB-D. Use the paper's 4 Hz timing as the reference, and log the camera exposure and robot-command timestamps.
2. Convert 15 Hz commands into action/state intervals at the sampled frame times. Integrate/resample the executed commands over each approximately 250 ms video interval; do not simply assign the 10-step chunk's first or last joint vector to a frame.
3. There is no faithful 54D-to-7D map that preserves both arms and both dexterous hands. For a controlled arm-only probe, map one arm's commanded joint targets to its relative end-effector xyz/rotation delta with forward kinematics, plus one grip scalar, and use the corresponding 7D end-effector state. This uses FK in the adapter, not as the pretrained model's general representation. Run the other arm in a separate model call only as a diagnostic; separate calls cannot represent coupled bimanual contacts.
4. For simultaneous bimanual use, add two typed action/state tokens (one per arm) or build a small dual-arm input adapter and train it while freezing the visual encoder and most predictor layers. This is a new conditioning layout, so report it as a fine-tuned transfer experiment, not zero-shot use. A 20-DoF hand cannot be represented faithfully by one scalar gripper token; for bottle grasping, test whether aperture/closure is sufficient, and keep a separate hand controller if the grasp depends on finger posture.
5. Fit only the action/state input projections and normalization initially. If the predictor remains action-insensitive on held-out pairs, try a small rank-2 LoRA in the final predictor blocks as a second stage, keeping a frozen-prior control and an action-free baseline. Use the paper's teacher-forcing plus short rollout objective as a starting point; add counterfactual or safe perturbation transitions if they are available. Do not turn on full CEM until one-step action sensitivity is established.
6. Evaluate with matched-initial-state pairs: hold observation fixed, vary one action coordinate or a short safe action sequence, then compare predicted and recorded changes in end-effector, hand, bottle pose, and contact/grasp state. Report directional accuracy, paired outcome-ranking accuracy, prediction error on tracked object/hand points, and open-loop degradation at 1/2/4+ steps. This is more diagnostic than average latent loss.

**Cost / risk.** Loading the checkpoint for frozen inference is the plausible H20 use case; actual inference VRAM is unpublished. A 20–40 GB free H20 may suffice for a single BF16 query but must be profiled with the exact implementation and clip length. The published CEM configuration is seconds per action on a 4090 and cannot run the 15 Hz control loop. The model's camera sensitivity and single-arm interface are the central risks.

### Option B — use a robot-agnostic latent action prior, then learn a 54D adapter

This better matches the preference to avoid robot-specific input coordinates. CLAP provides a 32D latent-action route and a frozen-world-model, zero-initialized MLP adapter. RynnWorld-Latent provides a 608D transition descriptor with motion/global-action/camera slices and demonstrates a 54D dual-hand target platform. LAC-WM uses a related shared latent space with a small explicit-action projector.

For our 84 demonstrations, derive the latent transition target from paired visual observations (and optionally point flow), then train an adapter from the 54D command interval to the latent-action descriptor while freezing the world model. Use the robot's action labels only to fit the adapter. Test whether predicted futures respond to the adapter output on held-out paired trajectories. Keep the latent action's camera component separate or suppress it if it mainly encodes view changes. If an explicit action decoder is needed, train it after the world model stage against the 54D commands; do not let that decoder quietly replace the dynamics prior.

**Cost / risk.** CLAP reports under 12 GB inference VRAM but about 1.49 s for 11 frames/25 denoising steps on H200; it is not a 15 Hz simulator. RynnWorld-Latent is much closer in embodiment, but its published target adaptation used 3,850 episodes and its visual action-following check is qualitative. The shared latent is not guaranteed to mean the same physically executable intervention for every robot; camera motion and appearance can leak into it. These are candidates for offline ranking, slow planning, or policy-data augmentation after a transfer probe, not an assumed fast low-level controller.

### Option C — keep the action interface in robot-agnostic 3D/flow space

If the priority is a robot-agnostic action meaning, evaluate an object/hand point-flow or 3DFlowAction-style representation. Use tracked object/hand points to represent intended motion, then map the selected target flow into end-effector trajectories and finally into the 54D controller. This allows the world model to predict physical motion in a common coordinate system and keeps joint semantics out of the visual predictor. The final controller is necessarily embodiment-specific. The risks are depth/segmentation/tracking error, occlusion, and loss of contact detail; the 3DFlowAction paper addresses these with camera-motion removal, depth lifting, rendered-flow checks, and task-specific gripper/IK logic.

### Option D — if adapting an action-free video dynamics prior, use layerwise action injection

DINO-world is the relevant pattern, not the October feature-only recipe: begin with a predictor pretrained on videos, add zero-initialized action residual blocks at multiple predictor layers, align each action to the frame it should affect, and tune only those blocks before considering full-model fine-tuning. Compare adapter-only with full fine-tuning and retain an action-free baseline. The published evidence is simulated and uses far more pretraining video than the 84 target demos, so keep it as a secondary controlled baseline rather than the primary transfer claim.

### Recommended order of work

1. **Action-faithfulness probe:** load V-JEPA 2-AC, freeze the prior, verify timing and one-arm EEF mapping, and run paired action sweeps. No goal scoring or CEM is needed for this decision.
2. **Bimanual interface probe:** compare (a) one-arm-at-a-time calls, (b) two typed EEF action/state tokens, and (c) a robot-agnostic latent action adapter. Use identical held-out sequences and keep RGB resolution/camera fixed.
3. **Small adaptation ladder:** train input adapters only; then rank-2 LoRA only if required; finally compare full predictor fine-tuning as an upper bound. Preserve the frozen checkpoint as a baseline.
4. **Contact-aware evaluation:** include both successes and failures, grasp/no-grasp outcomes, object pose and motion, plus short rollout error. The 84 successful demos alone cannot show what an unobserved alternative action would have caused.
5. **Only after that, plan:** use action-conditioned future scoring with a verified outcome metric. Given the team's 16/16 bottle-pushing failure, start with a small candidate set and an explicit conservative action prior. Do not use the current latent endpoint distance as the only objective.

## Inputs, memory, and transfer map for this robot

- **V-JEPA 2-AC image input:** 256×256 RGB, single view, sampled at 4 fps in the paper; ViT-g/16 makes a 16×16 patch grid. The paper's training clips are 16 frames; current official config says 8 frames. Pin and inspect the exact model/config before building the loader. No RGB-D input is documented.
- **V-JEPA 2-AC action/state:** 7D each, one arm: xyz + extrinsic Euler + gripper state, with action as an adjacent-sampled-frame delta; state is relative to robot base. Your 54D action is not a direct fit. If FK is used for an adapter, it maps each arm's joint command to a 6D EEF delta plus a grip scalar. A bimanual extension must represent both arms explicitly; a 54-to-7 MLP would erase one arm and most hand state.
- **Timing:** 10 commands × 1/15 s = 0.667 s. At 4 fps, that spans about 2.7 model intervals. Align commands to image exposure and aggregate over frame intervals. Preserve command duration and gripper transitions.
- **RGB-D:** none of V-JEPA 2-AC, V-JEPA 2.1, or DINO-WM uses an RGB-D channel in the cited setup. Begin with RGB to test the published interface. Depth can support a separate object-pose/flow evaluator or a later sensor-conditioned adapter.
- **Memory:** official V-JEPA 2-AC checkpoint artifact is 11.76 GB on disk; this is not its runtime VRAM. The paper/repo do not publish inference memory. Single-sample frozen BF16 inference on an H20 with 20–40 GB free is plausible but unverified. Profile a full observation window and predictor rollout first. The released post-training recipe requests 220 GB/GPU in its config, so reproducing its full distributed training is not compatible with the current H20 pool.
- **Latency:** 16 s/action is the paper's V-JEPA 2-AC CEM result (RTX 4090, horizon 1), not raw forward-pass latency. Do not confuse this with the robot's 15 Hz actuator rate. CLAP and other diffusion-video predictors also run much slower than 15 Hz in their published sampling setups.
- **Weights/licensing:** official V-JEPA 2 code has an MIT license; the checkpoint is publicly downloadable, but a separate weight licence is not explicit in the repository pages reviewed. Check the exact checkpoint terms before redistribution or commercial use.

## Open questions

1. Does V-JEPA 2-AC's action sensitivity survive the top Orbbec viewpoint, or does the camera-axis ambiguity dominate as in its appendix sweep?
2. Can its one-arm 7D representation be extended to two simultaneous arms without retraining the full predictor, and can a single scalar hand state preserve bottle grasp dynamics?
3. Are the 84 demonstrations varied enough in action timing and contact outcomes to train a reliable input adapter? How many paired safe perturbations or failure episodes are needed?
4. Does the model predict the object and hand transition accurately while the global image/latent loss remains unchanged? Which feature/object-point metric is most informative for contact?
5. Would a robot-agnostic latent action encode commanded manipulation separately from camera movement for a static top camera?
6. How should the 15 Hz/10-step command chunks be aligned to a 4 Hz model without losing grasp timing?
7. Can a predictor trained on successful demonstrations rank counterfactual outcomes, or is simulator/physical branching required as in OPENWAM?
8. What amount of adaptation is enough: input projections, per-arm token adapters, rank-2 LoRA, or full predictor fine-tuning?
9. How much of the reported transfer gains in large WAMs comes from action-conditioned dynamics versus visual pretraining, more task data, or action-head capacity?
10. Are the released V-JEPA 2-AC checkpoint terms sufficient for the intended deployment, given the repo's MIT code licence but no standalone weight licence text in the reviewed pages?
